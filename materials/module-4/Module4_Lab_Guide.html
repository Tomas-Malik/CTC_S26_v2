Search & Verify: Ground It, Extract It, Summarize It

Your step-by-step guide for all three beats. Work at your own laptop, alongside your AI assistant — this page tells you what to do, not what to type.

Level 1 No prerequisite Bring a laptop + AI assistant

“AI search doesn’t remove the work of finding things out — it relocates it. Your job stops being ‘search for the answer’ and becomes ‘verify what it found, and notice what it couldn’t have found.’”

The setup: you’re a volunteer researcher for the Elston Heights Bulletin, a neighborhood newsletter. Editor Dana Ruiz has a draft article — written by reporter Theo Marsh — about a development proposal headed for a city council vote in two days. Before it runs, Dana wants every figure in it checked against the real case file, not against whatever an AI assistant might already “know.” That’s the whole module: ground it, extract from it, and verify what you get back — three different skills, for three different kinds of question.

Beat One — Ground it

Goal: feel the difference between an AI assistant answering from memory and one answering from your actual documents.

  1. Ask first, before you give it anything
    Open a fresh AI chat with no documents attached. Ask about the Foundry on Elston project in Elston Heights, Chicago — how many units, what the affordability breakdown is, what happened at the most recent hearing.
  2. Notice what you got back
    Did it say it didn’t know? Did it answer anyway? Either is useful — just notice which, and whether you’d have any way to tell a confident wrong answer from a confident right one.
  3. Get the case file
    Download Module4_CaseFile_Starter.zip from the module page and unzip it. Inside: the fact sheets, hearing minutes, press release, comparison chart, Theo’s draft, and a plain-text bundle of everything except the chart image.
  4. Ground it, and ask the exact same question again
    Attach the documents to your AI assistant (or paste in the plain-text bundle if it can’t take file uploads). Ask the identical question from step 1. Compare the two answers side by side, and ask it to name which document each fact came from.

Worth noticing

Elston Heights and the Foundry on Elston aren’t real places — nothing your ungrounded assistant told you in step 1 could have been right by coincidence. That’s deliberate: most of what you’ll ever need to fact-check in real life is exactly this kind of thing — too specific, too local, or too recent for any AI to have learned it during training. Grounding isn’t optional polish for those cases. It’s the whole ballgame.

Beat Two — Extract it, check it

Goal: pull exact facts out of text, a table, and an image — then use them to actually do Dana’s assignment.

  1. Learn the technique: quote, then interpret
    For any fact you care about, ask your grounded assistant for two things separately: the exact source text it’s relying on (a verbatim quote), and its interpretation of that text. Put them side by side — if the interpretation claims more than the quote actually supports, that’s your signal.
  2. Pull three facts, three formats
    Get the exact quote from Alderwoman Voss announcing the vote result (it’s in running text). Get the exact affordable-unit count from the revised fact sheet (it’s in a table). Get the exact total-unit figure shown in the comparison chart (it’s in an image — if your tool won’t accept the image file, ask it what it would need from you instead).
  3. Now do the actual assignment: check Theo’s draft
    Open the draft article. Using what you just pulled — plus anything else you check — find every figure in the draft that doesn’t match the current case file. Go claim by claim; don’t accept or reject the whole article at once.
  4. Read it closely, not just against the other documents
    Some things are worth catching without cross-referencing anything at all. Read the draft on its own terms first.

If you think you've found it

Don’t just flag that something seems off — pin down exactly which figures are wrong, what the correct current figures are, and how you’d prove which one is current if someone pushed back on you. That last part is the actual skill.

Beat Three — Summarize it, verify that too

Goal: answer a question with no single verbatim sentence to check it against — and figure out how you’d verify it anyway.

  1. Ask a synthesis question
    Ask your grounded assistant: “In 2-3 sentences, why did Larkspur revise the proposal, and what changed?” Then, separately: “Summarize the overall community reaction at the July hearing.”
  2. Trace the answer back
    For each answer, ask yourself (or your assistant): what specific passage does each part of this trace back to? Flag anything the answer adds that you can’t trace to any document at all.
  3. Triangulate
    Ask the same question again with different wording. If part of the answer only shows up with one specific phrasing, treat that part as suspect.
  4. Check for the common shortcut
    A weak summary of the hearing usually just repeats the two people quoted by name and misses that four more residents spoke too. Check whether your answer did the same thing.

Why Beat Three doesn’t work like Beat Two

Beat Two had one right answer sitting in one place — you could check it against a quote. Beat Three’s answers are assembled from several documents at once, so there’s no single sentence to diff against. Verifying synthesis takes more judgment, not less — that’s true even when the assistant is perfectly grounded.

Two things to leave with

  • 1

    The searching got easy. The checking is the actual skill.

    Using AI search well is mostly the work of verifying what it surfaces, not the work of searching itself — and that work is invisible if you don’t know to look for it.

  • 2

    Watch for what it can't reach, not just what it gets wrong

    Over-relying on AI search trains you to stop looking anywhere it can’t reach — paywalled sources, internal documents, anything too recent to be indexed. You stop knowing what you don’t know, because the tool never shows you the shape of its own blind spot.

Keep your answers from all three beats around if you can — your facilitator may ask what you found and how you checked it, not just whether you finished.
