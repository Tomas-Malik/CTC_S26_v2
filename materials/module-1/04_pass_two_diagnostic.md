# Pass Two — Diagnostic Guide
*For facilitator use, and/or adapted into on-screen guidance if self-directed.*

## The task as given to the learner

"Same messy inputs as before. But now imagine the template from Pass One never
existed — you're brand new to this role, nobody left you an example, and you don't
actually know what Dana wants to see in a report like this. Use your AI assistant to
help you figure out *what should even go in this report* — before you generate it."

The instruction deliberately does not say "have a conversation first, then generate."
That's the actual thing being tested: will the learner structure first, or will they
default to the Pass One muscle memory and just generate immediately? Both are
realistic; the lesson works either way, but the diagnostic differs.

## The fork: did they structure first, or generate first?

**If they jumped straight to generating a report:**
This is not a failure to call out harshly — it's the single most useful teachable
moment in the whole module, because it's exactly what most people will do under real
deadline pressure. Likely result: a generic, template-shaped report that's full of
plausible-sounding section headers (something like "Summary / Key Metrics /
Challenges / Next Steps") but where the *content* sorted into those headers is
shakier than Pass One's, because nobody — human or AI — actually decided what
mattered to Dana specifically. It will look like a report. It may not be a *useful*
report.

Diagnostic question to ask: *"Does this look like a report, or does it tell Dana what
she actually needs to know? If you handed this to her with no other context, would
she know what needs her attention?"* Most learners will, on reflection, recognize the
gap themselves rather than needing to be told.

Then: have them go back and run the structuring conversation (see below) *before*
regenerating, and compare the two outputs side by side. The comparison is the lesson,
not a lecture about it.

**If they structured first:**
Good — check the structuring conversation itself, not just the eventual report. Three
things worth checking:

1. **Did they tell AI what Dana cares about, or did they ask AI to guess?** A
   structuring conversation that says "what sections should a nonprofit program
   report have" will get a generic, defensible-but-context-free answer. A better one
   gives AI something to work with: "Dana is the program director, this goes in a
   board packet, she's mainly going to scan it for anything that needs her decision
   or attention versus routine good news — what structure would serve that?"

2. **Did the structure leave room for "I don't know yet" or "this needs a decision,"
   not just "wins" and "metrics"?** A common failure here is a structure that's all
   positive-framing (Highlights, Achievements, Numbers) with nowhere for the
   Denise situation or the placement-number correction to honestly live. If the
   structure has no place for unresolved or ambiguous things, that's worth surfacing
   — it usually means the structuring conversation didn't ask AI to think about what
   the *reader* needs, only about what categories reports "usually" have.

3. **Did they sanity-check the structure against the actual messy data before
   generating, or did they pick a structure in the abstract and hope it fit?** Best
   practice (worth modeling explicitly if nobody does it spontaneously): paste in the
   messy inputs *as part of* the structuring conversation — "here's what I actually
   have to work with, does this structure still make sense, or does something not
   fit?" — rather than designing a structure in a vacuum and forcing data into it
   afterward.

## What "good" looks like here, in general terms (not a fixed answer)

There is no single right structure — that's the point of this pass, and worth saying
explicitly to the group so nobody assumes they got it "wrong" for landing somewhere
different. But a structure that's working well will usually:

- Have somewhere for things that are good news, separate from things that need
  attention or a decision — because conflating them is how urgent things get missed.
- Have somewhere for genuine uncertainty (the Denise situation, the corrected
  placement number, the unconfirmed rumor) that doesn't force false resolution.
- Reflect *who's reading it and why*, not just "what sections does a report usually
  have."

If a learner's structure does all three but looks nothing like Pass One's template —
that's success, not a deviation to correct.

## Facilitator / guided-diagnosis flow

1. Ask first, before looking at anything: "Did you have a back-and-forth with the AI
   about structure, or did you ask it to write the report directly?" Self-report,
   not inspection — this builds the habit of noticing your own process, which is the
   actual transferable skill.
2. Walk through the three structuring-conversation checks above if they structured
   first; or run the compare-after-the-fact exercise if they didn't.
3. Close with the naming moment: "When there's no example to anchor to, the AI can
   still help — but only if you treat it as a thinking partner on the structure
   *before* asking it to fill that structure in. Skipping straight to generation
   isn't wrong, it's just usually slower in the end, because you end up fixing
   structure and content at the same time instead of one at a time."

## A starter structuring-conversation opener (a direction, not a script)

If a learner doesn't know how to start the structuring conversation at all, this is a
reasonable opener to suggest:

> "I need to write a monthly report for my manager, but I don't have a template to
> work from. Before we write anything, help me think through: given that this goes
> to a nonprofit program director who's mainly scanning for things that need her
> attention or a decision versus routine good news, what categories or sections would
> actually serve that? I'll paste in what I actually have to report on once we've
> got a structure, so we can check it holds up against the real material."

Same caveat as Pass One: this is a direction to nudge toward, not a model answer to
hand over verbatim.
